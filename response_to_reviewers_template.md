### Point 1.2: Clarifying the Engagement with and Boundary Conditions of the Network Variety Model

> **Reviewer 1:**  
> "What is lacking here is even a rough outline of the theoretical framework for the structural foundations of cultural activity, i.e. an explanation of why and which non-occupational divisions within social networks might ‘influence’ an individual’s participation in various types of activities. It seems more plausible to assume that the ideological composition of the network will be linked in some way to the profile of cultural participation, but once again there is no indication of a theoretical link connecting the two aspects."

> **Reviewer 2:**  
> "I would like to see the authors give their central contribution a little more room. For me, the most interesting finding is that different kinds of connections relate differently to different forms of participation. Strong ties appear especially relevant to public participation, while the pattern for everyday leisure is quite different. This gives the paper something substantive to say about how cultural participation is socially organized. At times, however, that insight gets somewhat buried under the several gaps the paper proposes to fill (which is a bit of stretch; the authors can decide) I would bring these differences forward earlier and use them to connect the discussion of network variety, composition, and participation. The paper has several good ideas, but I sometimes wanted a clearer sense of which one the authors most wanted readers to take away."

> **Reviewer 2:**  
> "I appreciate that the paper engages seriously with the Network Variety Model and tries to extend a productive research tradition. I was less sure about exactly where the findings challenge the model. The manuscript moves between cultural familiarity, taste breadth, participation breadth, and participation frequency, and these are not quite the same thing. Someone might become familiar with more cultural forms without participating in each one more frequently. That seems particularly relevant here because the outcomes are factor scores constructed from activity frequencies. I would clarify which proposition of the model is being examined. To my mind, showing that network variety has different associations across activity domains is already interesting; the paper does not need to establish that the broader variety principle fails."

## Cross-Cutting Theme 2: Conceptualizing and Disentangling Network Variety vs. Network Composition

### Point 2.1: Terminological Distinction: "Variety" vs. "Composition" vs. "Heterogeneity"

> **Reviewer 4:**  
> "The paper wants to distinguish between network “variety” (“volume, range, and heterogeneity of ties) and network “composition” (“the extent to which personal networks are structured around particular sociodemographic or ideological groups”). These two terms are too closely related to each other. Variety is synonymous with heterogeneity, which is a compositional quality. This terminology makes the argument difficult to follow and will likely confuse other readers."  
> "The definition of network variety confounds several distinct aspects of networks (size, structural connectedness, and heterogeneity). It’s not clear why they should be combined together."

> **Reviewer 4:**  
> "The paper interprets network Factor 1 as “network variety” because it loads across a broad number of social categories. As I mentioned above, the paper’s definition of “network variety” conflates a number of different dimensions of networks. This factor may be interpretable as network volume or (in the SAOM literature sense) “expansiveness,” but I don’t see why we should interpret it as a structural connectedness (range) measure."

> **Reviewer 2:**  
> "The group-based network measure is a promising feature of the study. I would nevertheless explain more fully why the first factor is interpreted as general network variety."

## Cross-Cutting Theme 3: Measurement Model, Factor Analysis, and Latent Scale Construction

### Point 3.1: Factor Extraction Method: PCA vs. Common Factor Analysis / Latent Variable Modeling

> **Reviewer 1:**  
> "I have some reservations about the interpretation of the principal component analysis results for the variables relating to cultural practices and leisure time. Whilst the first of the identified factors (“public arts participation”) appears to be more consistent, the second, termed “solitary leisure activities”, is not. Substantially it covers “intellectual” activities such as reading novels or going to the library with body-related activities (e.g. “go to the gym, to exercise, or do yoga”, “go for a walk”). Please also note the fairly significant differences in factor loadings and the phenomenon of cross-loadings. This may suggest a lack of consistency amongst the explained variables. I would suggest checking the reliability of the indicators using Cronbach’s alpha and/or McDonald’s omega, as well as examining the results of the principal component analysis when changing the settings, including allowing for oblique rotation. Confirmatory factor analysis would also provide a more reliable measurement model of types of cultural participation."

> **Reviewer 1:**  
> "The results and interpretation of the principal component analysis for the network indicators also raise doubts in my mind. Is it really the case that the first factor reflects the network variety, given the many low factor loadings? Second and third factors are more plausible, but the also mix not obvious items (e.g., knowing very liberal people with Hispanic/Latin contacts). Please make robustness checks (e.g., by using alternative reduction techniques or reliability analyses) to ensure that constructed explanatory variables are reliable."

> **Reviewer 4:**  
> "The key constructs both for cultural participation and network are measured by extracting factors from a “principal components factor analysis.” First, as a terminological point, principal components extraction does not separate common from unique variance; it is not a common factor model."  
> "- While PCA and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) are valuable for exploratory analysis and good first steps for understanding dimensionality, they are unsuitable for constructing the final scale scores because the factor scores are based on every item instead of just those that are (a) theoretically relevant (i.e., have construct validity) and (b) have clear evidence of loading onto the same factor."  
> "- Consequently, the paper should use a more appropriate latent variable technique for scale construction that is theory-driven with a factor structure informed by an exploratory technique like PCA or EFA. Defensible choices include a confirmatory factor model designed for ordinal, non-continuous indicators or an item response model like the graded response model or partial credit model. Such models need to be statistically evaluated for adequacy."

**Location in Revised Manuscript:** [e.g., Section 3.3: Measurement Models and Scale Construction, pp. X–Y; Appendix Tables A1–A3]  
**Response:**  
[Insert response addressing factor extraction, reporting scale reliability metrics (Cronbach's alpha, McDonald's omega), comparing PCA with common factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and demonstrating the robustness of substantive conclusions.]

### Point 4.3: Confounding Dispositions, Personality, and "Overselling" Mediation

> **Reviewer 3:**  
> "Specifically, the authors conclude in the Abstract that “our study shows that the association between political ideology (liberal or conservative) and cultural engagement is fully explained by the ideological makeup of personal networks ... rather than solely via political beliefs and related psychological dispositions.” In other words, partisans adopt the lifestyles they do because they are imitating the co-partisans in their networks, not because of any intrinsic traits such as curiosity or a lack thereof. For reasons I will elaborate on, this seems like a conclusion that goes well beyond the data."

> **Reviewer 3:**  
> "But it also feels a bit unsatisfying and incomplete. The conclusion strikes me as similar to the finding of DellaPosta et al’s “Why do Liberals Drink Lattes?,” which I think failed to answer its titular question in a meaningful way. It concludes that liberals drink lattes because other liberals do, which, despite the impressive statistical methods, borders on the tautological. Sure, conformity is surely a factor, but how did that specific preference emerge to begin with? Why aren’t liberals instead drinking sweet tea, Diet Coke, or chocolate milk, which then spreads in popularity through cultural transmission? Was the initial preference for lattes arbitrary? To say that the relationship between ideology and engagement is “completely explained” or “fully mediated” by network composition may be true of the manuscript’s model, but that would just indicate a facial insufficiency of the model. A more complete theory would need to explain how those initial politicultural differences became prominent enough to be noticed and imitated in the first place. The present project doesn’t need to be that comprehensive, but it also can’t claim to be when it isn’t."

> **Reviewer 3:**  
> "Part of my objection may simply be rooted in the authors “overselling” their argument. It seems like a dramatic overstatement to suggest in the Abstract that the model rules out personality influences, when personality wasn’t a variable in the model. And theoretically why shouldn’t a dispositional trait like Openness to Experience lead to both social extraversion and cultural curiosity?"

**Location in Revised Manuscript:** [e.g., Abstract; Section 2; Section 5: Discussion, pp. X–Y]  
**Response:**  
[Insert response removing assertions that personality or intrinsic traits are ruled out, reframing the network mediation finding as relational mediation within model bounds, and qualifying theoretical claims about the origin of politicultural cleavages.]

## Cross-Cutting Theme 5: Empirical Modeling, Counterintuitive Findings, and Missing Interactions

### Point 5.1: Interpreting Substantively Counterintuitive Regression Findings

> **Reviewer 1:**  
> "As a consequence of the insufficient theoretical grounding of the relationships between network variables and cultural participation in the first part of the manuscript, the empirical and concluding sections do not attempt to explain the sometimes surprising relationships between the variables. Why is strong-tie network diversity a better predictor of public arts participation than weak-tie network diversity? In other words: Why does the strength of ties here matter? Why is weak-tie liberal network negatively associated with public arts participation? Why do strong ties to people of conservative profile connect to higher levels of public arts participation?"  
> "Similar questions should be raised when it comes to solitary leisure. Why is weak-tie variety positively associated with this outcome variable, whereas strong-tie variety is negatively associated? Why are ties to people of liberal profile more conducive to solitary leisure rather than ties to more conservative people. I suspect that this correlation may stem from the fact that yoga – which is associated with people who hold more liberal views – was included amongst the leisure activities. Please add a discussion of the theoretical mechanisms that would explain the observed correlations."

> **Reviewer 3:**  
> "Another example: why should weak ties to liberals decrease cultural engagement, but strong ties to conservatives increase it? That’s counterintuitive (at least to me), so a better proposed explanation would be welcome. It feels insufficient to say, as the manuscript does on page 23, that conservatives “increasingly appreciate and defend” highbrow culture. The authors don’t cite anything for that proposition, other than a 2016 study – that’s ten years old, so can’t really speak to “increasingly.” And it’s pre-Trump – presumably the authors would not characterize the MAGA movement as a highbrow bunch of people?"  
> "The explanation on pp.18-19 feels similarly unhelpful: “Overall, it seems that, given the strong positive effect of variety in strong ties on more active forms of arts participation, they function here as a mechanism to funnel people into those more public pursuits, thereby depressing participation in competing solitary leisure activities. Weak ties, in contrast, given their less demanding nature, facilitate involvement across the entire range of leisure and cultural consumption activities.” To me, that doesn’t illuminate the question of why a close friend might be likelier to turn me on to the opera than to gardening. Without a stronger theoretical explanation, the findings (which are myriad and sometimes counter-intuitive) risk being seen as fragmented relics of measurement/sampling error rather than true insights."
